Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) Attacks

A Denial of Service (DoS) attack tries to make a website, server or network unavailable to legitimate users by overwhelming it with traffic or requests. When the attack traffic comes from many different machines at the same time, it is called a Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack. The attacking machines are usually part of a botnet, which is a network of compromised computers, routers and Internet of Things (IoT) devices controlled by the attacker.

DDoS attacks fall into three main types. Volumetric attacks, such as UDP floods and DNS amplification, try to consume all available bandwidth. Protocol attacks, such as the SYN flood, exhaust the resources of servers, firewalls or load balancers by abusing how network protocols work. Application-layer attacks, also called Layer 7 attacks, send large numbers of seemingly normal HTTP requests to expensive pages such as search or login, which makes them harder to tell apart from real users.

Amplification attacks exploit services that send a much larger response than the request they receive. The attacker spoofs the victim's IP address, so the large responses are sent to the victim.

Signs of a DDoS attack include a sudden spike in traffic from many IP addresses, a website becoming very slow or unreachable, and unusual traffic patterns such as many requests to a single endpoint.

Mitigation includes using a cloud-based DDoS protection service or Content Delivery Network (CDN) that can absorb large volumes of traffic, rate limiting, a Web Application Firewall (WAF) to filter malicious Layer 7 requests, and having extra capacity available. Organisations should also prepare a DDoS response plan with their internet service provider in advance.
